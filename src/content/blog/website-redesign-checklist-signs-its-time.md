Most business websites do not fail all at once. They drift: the design ages, the phone layout gets awkward, a few pages go out of date, and one day you realise nobody has sent an enquiry in months.

A redesign is a big decision, and it is not always the right one. This checklist helps you tell whether you need one, and how to do it without losing what is already working.

## Ten signs it is time

**1. It looks dated.** Visitors judge a business in the first few seconds. If your site looks like it was made years ago, they may assume the business is not as current as it is.

**2. It is hard to use on a phone.** Many visitors arrive on a phone. If text is tiny, buttons are hard to tap or menus are awkward, they leave.

**3. It is slow.** Pages that take a long time to appear lose people before they see anything. Google publishes Core Web Vitals, a set of measures of loading, responsiveness and layout stability, and you can check yours for free in PageSpeed Insights.

**4. Visitors do not know what to do next.** If a stranger cannot tell within a few seconds what you do and what to do about it, the page is not doing its job.

**5. It is not bringing enquiries.** If you get visitors but few calls, bookings or messages, something between arriving and acting is broken.

**6. It is hard to update.** If changing a price, a photo or your opening hours needs a developer, it will not get done, and the site will fall out of date.

**7. The information is out of date or wrong.** Old hours, old prices, broken links and missing contact details make a business look closed.

**8. It does not look like your business any more.** The brand, services or customers have changed, and the site has not.

**9. It does not show up in search.** Thin pages, a confusing structure and missing basics make it hard for search engines to understand what the site is about.

**10. You are embarrassed to send people to it.** If you avoid putting your web address on a flyer or in an email, that is your answer.

If you recognised two or three of these, you may only need fixes. If you recognised five or more, a redesign is probably worth planning.

## What to keep

A redesign is not a reason to throw everything away. Before anything is rebuilt, decide what is worth keeping:

- **Pages that already bring visitors.** Check which pages people find through search, and keep their content and the substance of their address.
- **Text that works.** Good descriptions and answers to common questions can usually be edited, not rewritten.
- **Real photos.** Photos of your own premises, team and work are worth more than new stock images.
- **Reviews and testimonials.** Keep every real one, with permission.
- **Your domain name.** A redesign changes how the site looks and works, not your address.

## What to change

- A clear first screen: what you do, who it is for, and one next step.
- A layout designed for phones first.
- Fast pages, with images sized properly.
- A structure where each service or topic has its own page.
- A way for you to update the things that change.

## Do not lose your search rankings

This is where redesigns go wrong. When a page moves to a new address and nothing points the old one to the new one, the search visibility it earned is lost.

Before launch, ask whoever is building the site to:

1. **List every address on the current site**, and which ones get visitors.
2. **Plan a redirect for each one**, pointing the old address to the best new page.
3. **Keep titles, headings and useful text** on pages that already do well.
4. **Record your numbers first**: visitors, enquiries and your main search terms, so you can compare afterwards.
5. **Check after launch** for broken links and for pages that have dropped out of search.

Nobody can promise a redesign will keep rankings exactly as they were. A plan like this is how you give them the best chance, and how you will know if something goes wrong.
