Ask five people what a business website costs and you will get five answers, from "a few hundred" to "tens of thousands." They are not all lying. They are describing very different things under the same word.

This guide explains what drives the price, so you can tell which kind of website you are being quoted for, and whether the number makes sense.

## What the published guides say

Cost guides that price websites in US dollars vary a great deal, but they agree on the shape. As examples from their published guides:

- [WebFX](https://www.webfx.com/blog/web-design/website-costs/) lists roughly **$500 to $10,000 or more** for a small business site built by a freelancer, and roughly **$3,000 to $30,000 or more** for an agency.
- [Liquid Web](https://www.liquidweb.com/blog/website-design-cost/) lists roughly **$2,000 to $50,000 or more** for an online store.
- [Seahawk Media](https://seahawkmedia.com/guides/smb/how-much-does-a-website-cost/) notes that ongoing plans for small business sites commonly run from about **$15 to $100 a month**.

Treat these as rough guides, not quotes. They come from companies that sell websites, prices in the UK and Europe differ, and a range this wide mostly tells you that the work being priced is not the same work.

## What actually drives the price

**1. How many pages and how much content.** Five pages and fifty pages are different projects. So is a site where you supply all the text and photos and one where someone has to write and source them.

**2. Custom design or a template.** A template is a design someone else made that is adjusted for you. Custom design is made from scratch for your business. Templates cost less and look like other sites using the same template. Custom costs more and fits you.

**3. What the site has to do.** A site that explains your business is simple. Add booking, online ordering, a members area, a search of properties or a shop, and every feature is extra work, extra testing and often a monthly fee to a service provider.

**4. Integrations.** Connecting to a booking system, a payment provider, a CRM or a property feed means working to someone else's rules. It is one of the most common reasons a quote grows.

**5. Who builds it.** A student, a freelancer, a studio and a large agency price very differently, and they bring different amounts of experience, process and support.

**6. How fast you need it.** A compressed timeline usually costs more, because other work has to move out of the way.

## The costs people forget

The price of the build is not the whole cost of having a website.

- **Domain name.** Paid every year.
- **Hosting.** Some builds need paid hosting, and some can be hosted cheaply or free. Ask which.
- **Maintenance and updates.** Someone has to keep the site working, patched and backed up. Ask whether it is included, and for how long.
- **Content.** Writing and photography are often not in the quote. If you cannot supply them, budget for them.
- **Changes after launch.** Ask what happens when you want a new page six months from now, and what it costs.
- **Third-party services.** Booking tools, email tools and payment providers often charge monthly or take a share of sales.

## How to compare quotes fairly

Two quotes are only comparable if they cover the same work. Before you compare prices, get each one to say:

1. How many pages, and which ones.
2. Whether the design is custom or a template.
3. What features are included, and what is not.
4. Who writes the text and supplies the photos.
5. What happens at launch: testing on phones, search setup, redirects from an old site.
6. What you pay after launch, and for what.
7. Who owns the finished site and the code, and what you receive at handover.
8. How many rounds of changes are included.

A quote that does not answer these is a price with the important parts missing.

## Warning signs

- **A price with no written scope.** If it is not written down, it will change.
- **A very low price that hides later costs.** We wrote about [why cheap websites often cost more later](/blog/hidden-cost-of-cheap-websites/).
- **No questions about your business.** Someone who prices your site without asking who it is for is pricing a template.
- **No clear answer on ownership.** You should know what you own when the project ends.
- **Pressure to decide today.** A good studio is comfortable with you taking a few days.

## The bottom line

There is no single price for a business website, because there is no single kind. The useful question is not "how much does a website cost?" but "what exactly will this one do, who builds it, and what will it cost me over the next two years?"
