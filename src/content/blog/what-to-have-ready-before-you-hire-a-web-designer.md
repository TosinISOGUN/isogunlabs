When a website project runs late, the cause is rarely the design or the code. It is usually something the business owner did not have ready: the text for the About page, photos that are good enough to use, a login for the domain nobody can find, or a decision that three people have to agree on.

None of that is a failure. Most business owners have never commissioned a website before, and nobody hands you a list. This is that list. If you have these things in hand before you start, the work moves faster, the result is closer to what you pictured, and there are fewer surprises on the invoice.

We wrote about [why cheap websites often cost more later](/blog/hidden-cost-of-cheap-websites/). This is the practical other side: what you can do up front to make any website project go well.

## 1. Know what the website is for

Before pages and colors, decide the one thing you most want a visitor to do. Call you, book an appointment, send an enquiry, place an order, or find your address. Most sites try to do five things and do none of them clearly.

Write it as a single sentence: "I want people who find us on Google to contact us about X." Everything else on the site should support that sentence, and anyone building for you will ask for it.

## 2. List the pages you need

Start small. A typical business site needs:

- **Home.** What you do, who it is for, and what to do next.
- **Services or products.** What you offer, in plain language.
- **About.** Who you are, and why someone should trust you.
- **Contact.** How to reach you, and a form if you want one.

Beyond that, add pages only when they have a reason to exist. A page you will not keep up to date is worse than no page. If you are unsure, write the list and mark each page as "must have" or "later." It keeps the scope clear and stops the project growing quietly.

## 3. Prepare your words

This is the item that delays projects more than any other. Someone has to write the text, and a web designer is not always the right person, because they do not know your customers or your business the way you do.

For each page, gather:

- What you want to say, even if it is rough notes.
- The questions customers ask you most often. These make good page content.
- Facts that build trust: how long you have been operating, areas you serve, qualifications, and real customer feedback that you have permission to use.
- Your contact details, written exactly as they should appear.

Do not worry about polish. It is much easier to edit existing words than to start from a blank page, so rough notes in your own voice are better than nothing.

## 4. Gather your photos and logo

Good images matter more than most people expect, and they are hard to fake. Stock photos are fine for some uses, but visitors can tell when every picture could belong to any business.

Collect:

- **Your logo**, ideally as a vector file or a large, clean image.
- **Photos of your work, your premises, and your team.** Recent, well lit, and taken at a decent resolution. A modern phone is usually good enough if the light is good.
- **Anything you have the right to use.** If a photo was taken by someone else, make sure you are allowed to publish it.

If you have none, decide early whether to arrange a photo session. It is better to find that out in the first week than the last.

## 5. Sort out your domain name

Your domain is the address of your website, such as `yourbusiness.com`. Two questions are worth answering before the project starts:

**Do you already own one?** If you do, find out where it is registered and who has the login. This is the single most common holdup. The account is often in the name of a former employee or a previous developer.

**If you do not, who will register it?** Whoever does it, make sure the domain is registered in your business's name and that you hold the account details. A domain registered under a freelancer's or agency's personal account can become a problem if you ever part ways.

The same applies to hosting. You should know who is paying for it, who controls the account, and when it renews. Ask for this in writing before the work begins.

## 6. Decide who makes the decisions

Projects stall when feedback arrives from five people who disagree. Before you start, pick one person who has the final say, and let them collect opinions internally.

Agree up front on how many rounds of changes are included and how feedback should be sent. A single, consolidated list of changes is far easier to act on than a trail of separate messages.

## 7. Collect examples you like, and ones you do not

Show, do not describe. Send a few websites you admire, and say what you like about each: the layout, the tone, the way the menu works. Send one or two you dislike, and say why.

This takes ten minutes and prevents more misunderstandings than any amount of written briefing. You are not asking anyone to copy another site, only to understand your taste.

## 8. Be clear about money and time

Agree on these before work starts, and get them in writing:

- **The total cost**, and what it includes. Pages, revisions, domain, hosting, and any setup work.
- **What is not included**, and how extra work is quoted. If something is outside the original scope, you should hear the price before it is built, not after.
- **When payments are due.** Many projects use a deposit at the start and the balance on delivery.
- **How long it should take**, and what the timeline depends on. Usually that is you supplying text and photos on time.
- **What happens after launch.** Who fixes small problems, for how long, and what changes after that.
- **Who owns the finished site**, and when ownership passes to you.

A good builder will be glad to answer these. If someone is vague about them, treat that as information.

## A short checklist

Before you contact anyone, tick off as many of these as you can:

- One sentence on what the site is for
- A list of pages, marked must-have or later
- Rough text for each page
- Logo and a folder of usable photos
- Domain login, or a decision on who will register one
- One person with final say
- Three examples of sites you like
- A budget and a timeline you are comfortable with

You do not need all of them to begin. Every one you have ready is a conversation you do not have to have halfway through the project, and a day off the schedule.

## The bottom line

A website is built from two things: the skill of the person making it and the material you give them. You cannot control the first once you have chosen well, but you control the second entirely. Arriving prepared is the cheapest improvement you can make to any web project.
