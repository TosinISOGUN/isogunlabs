Choosing a web design company is hard because the sales pages all sound the same. Every studio is "creative," "results-driven," and "passionate." None of that helps you decide who will build a site that works for your business and still works two years from now.

The fix is to stop comparing slogans and start asking the same specific questions of every company you speak to. The answers, and how easily they come, tell you almost everything. This guide gives you twelve of them, what a good answer sounds like, and what should make you pause.

## The short version

If you only have a minute, ask these before you sign anything:

- **Who** will actually build my site, and who will I talk to?
- **What exactly** is included, in writing, and what is not?
- **Who owns** the domain, the hosting account and the code?
- **How fast** will the finished site load, on a phone?
- **What happens** after launch, and what does that cost?

The rest of this guide goes through these and seven more in detail.

## 1. Who will actually build my website, and who will I talk to?

Some companies sell the project and hand the work to someone you never meet. That is not always a problem, but you should know it. Ask who designs, who writes the code, and who your day-to-day contact is.

**A good answer:** names the roles, says who you will talk to, and explains how questions reach the person doing the work.

**Pause if:** the answer is vague, or "the team" is never described.

## 2. Can I see work like mine?

Look at the studio's portfolio on your phone, not only on a laptop. Do the sites load quickly? Do they look finished on a small screen? Is the work for businesses like yours, or only for a different kind of customer?

Be careful with samples that are not real client projects. A good studio will say plainly which pieces are live client sites and which are concept designs. Both can be fine, as long as you are told which is which.

**A good answer:** shows relevant work, explains what the studio did on each project, and is honest about what is a concept.

**Pause if:** the portfolio is a wall of screenshots with no context.

## 3. What exactly is included, in writing?

A friendly chat is not a scope. Ask for a written plan that lists the pages, the features, the number of design revisions, the timeline and the price. Without it, the first disagreement will be about what "a website" meant.

If you are still working out what you need, our guide to [what to have ready before you hire a web designer](/blog/what-to-have-ready-before-you-hire-a-web-designer/) will help you write a clearer brief.

**A good answer:** a plan and a quote you can read in one sitting, with clear lines around what is extra.

**Pause if:** the price arrives before anyone has asked what the site is for.

## 4. Who owns the website, the domain and the code?

This one question prevents the most painful surprises. You should be the registered owner of your domain name. You should hold the login to your hosting account, or have a clear written arrangement about it. And you should know whether you receive the site's files and code when the project ends.

**A good answer:** the domain is registered in your name, you get the logins at handover, and the studio explains how you can move the site elsewhere if you ever want to.

**Pause if:** the studio registers everything under its own account and the contract says nothing about handing it over.

## 5. How will it be built: a template or custom work?

Neither is automatically better. A well-chosen template can be a sensible, affordable start for a small business. Custom work costs more but can fit your brand and your goals more closely. The problem is not the approach, it is not knowing which one you are paying for.

**A good answer:** explains the approach, why it suits your project, and what it means for cost and for changes later.

**Pause if:** a custom price is quoted for what is really a template with new colors.

## 6. How fast will the finished site be?

Speed affects how visitors feel about your business, and Google measures it. Google's guidance on [Core Web Vitals](https://web.dev/articles/vitals) sets out targets for a good experience, measured for at least 75 percent of visits:

- **Loading:** the main content should appear within **2.5 seconds** (Largest Contentful Paint).
- **Interactivity:** the page should respond to taps and clicks within **200 milliseconds** (Interaction to Next Paint).
- **Visual stability:** the layout should not jump around as it loads, with a score of **0.1 or less** (Cumulative Layout Shift).

You can check any site, including the studio's own and the ones in its portfolio, for free with [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/).

**A good answer:** the studio talks about speed as part of the build, not as an afterthought, and can show you how it measures it.

**Pause if:** speed is "something we can look at later," or the studio's own site is slow.

## 7. Will it work well on phones and for people with disabilities?

Many of your visitors will find you on a phone, so the mobile version is the real website, not a smaller copy of it. Ask how the studio designs and tests for small screens.

Ask about accessibility too: readable text, enough color contrast, keyboard navigation and image descriptions. The [WCAG guidelines](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/) are the widely used standard, and in some regions accessibility is also a legal expectation for businesses.

**A good answer:** mobile comes first in the design, and accessibility basics are part of the work, not an optional extra.

**Pause if:** the answer to "do you test on real phones?" is a pause.

## 8. How do you handle SEO, and what do you promise?

Good websites are built so search engines can understand them: clear page titles and descriptions, sensible headings, fast loading, clean links, a sitemap, and redirects if you are replacing an old site. Google's own [SEO starter guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide) covers the fundamentals.

What nobody can honestly promise is a ranking. Search results depend on competition, content and time. Be wary of anyone who guarantees you will be number one.

**A good answer:** lists the practical SEO work built into the project and is realistic about results.

**Pause if:** the promise is a ranking, or the company has no plan for redirecting your old pages.

## 9. What will it cost, and what costs come after?

Compare quotes on what they include, not on the total alone. Ask about hosting, domain renewals, security updates, changes after launch, and any monthly plan. Published guides put business websites anywhere from a few hundred dollars to tens of thousands, because they describe very different work, as we explain in [how much a business website costs](/blog/how-much-does-a-business-website-cost/).

A very low price is not always a bargain either. The [hidden cost of cheap websites](/blog/hidden-cost-of-cheap-websites/) shows where the savings tend to disappear.

**A good answer:** an itemized quote with the ongoing costs spelled out.

**Pause if:** the ongoing costs only come up after you have signed.

## 10. What is the timeline, and what do you need from me?

Projects slow down when the business owner's text, photos or decisions arrive late. A good studio tells you what it needs, when, and what happens to the schedule if it is delayed.

**A good answer:** a timeline with milestones and a clear list of what you must provide and by when.

**Pause if:** the only date you hear is the launch date.

## 11. What happens after the site goes live?

Launch is the start of the site's life, not the end of the project. Ask who fixes problems, who handles updates and security, how long after launch the studio will fix mistakes at no charge, and how you can make changes yourself. A good handover includes a short walkthrough of how to run the site.

**A good answer:** a defined support period, a clear price for changes, and a handover that lets you manage everyday updates.

**Pause if:** "support" means "send us an email and we will see."

## 12. How will we communicate, especially across time zones?

Many good studios work remotely, and your studio may be in a different country. That can work very well, provided communication is planned. Ask how updates are shared, how often you will see progress, which language and channels are used, and whether calls can happen at hours that suit you.

**A good answer:** a named contact, written updates, a working preview you can open any time, and calls at times that suit your time zone.

**Pause if:** you are not sure who to message when something goes wrong.

## Red flags at a glance

- No written plan or quote before work starts
- Guaranteed rankings or "number one on Google"
- The studio keeps the domain, hosting or code
- No mention of mobile, speed or accessibility
- No contact name, and answers that change from one email to the next
- Pressure to sign today

## Frequently asked questions

### How many web design companies should I talk to?

Two or three is enough for most small businesses. Ask each the same questions from this guide, then compare their answers and their written plans side by side.

### Is the cheapest quote the one to avoid?

Not always, but check what is missing. A low price that leaves out the ongoing costs, the handover or the speed work can end up more expensive than a fuller quote.

### Should I hire a freelancer or a studio?

Either can do good work. A freelancer is often more affordable for a simple site. A studio brings several skills together and a backup if one person is unavailable. What matters is the answers to the twelve questions above.

### What should I prepare before I contact a studio?

Know what the site is for, which pages you need, and what text and photos you already have. Our checklist on [what to have ready before you hire a web designer](/blog/what-to-have-ready-before-you-hire-a-web-designer/) walks through it.

### Can a website company guarantee I will rank on Google?

No honest company can. A good one builds the site so search engines can understand it and loads quickly, and is realistic that rankings depend on competition, content and time.
